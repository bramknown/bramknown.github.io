Crisis in the Honey Industry

Economic & Operational Pressures

  • Time Consuming – Weekly manual hive inspections
  • Labor Intensive – Labour can consume a large share of operating budgets
  • Disturbance – Opening hives stresses colonies and weakens them

Major Biological Threats

Small Hive Beetles

Invasive pests that destroy comb and contaminate honey.

American Foulbrood

Highly contagious bacterial disease that can wipe out colonies.

Varroa Mite

Parasitic mite that vectors viruses and is a leading cause of colony loss.

Traditional monitoring relies on beekeepers suiting up, opening hives weekly, and checking for signs of trouble. Early detection is often too late, leading to massive kill-offs and economic devastation for farmers who depend on healthy pollination.

Bram's - Interview Focus Research

Interviews conducted and explored:

  • User groups: end-users (commercial & hobbyist beekeepers), stakeholders, and inspirational experts.
  • Assumptions before interviews versus reflections after (Report, Relate, Reason, Reconstruct).
  • Key concerns around labour, disease detection, cost willingness-to-pay, and trust in new technology.

Research Interviews

Celine — End User / Beekeeper, Owns and manages one beehive

  • Checks hive condition every two weeks.
  • Needs to assess financial situation before buying technical products.
  • Checks varroa mites every 1-2 weeks and uses chemicals; pest management is a routine burden.
  • Wants affordable bee protection products; clear demand exists.
  • Pests remain a problem, so technology must target multiple pest types.
  • Average honey production is about 50 kg per hive per year.

Vijaya — Inspirator / Analytics & IT Specialist, AI and computer vision expert.

  • Machine learning for mite detection requires high-end equipment.
  • The detection software can be installed and used without farmers directly operating the equipment.
  • Computer vision with high accuracy and low latency will be crucial.
  • Current entrance data may be insufficient; new images with varied backgrounds are needed.
  • Software should run on lower-performance hardware to be more accessible.
  • Cost-effective versions are possible and meet demand for affordable tools.

Melanie — End User / experienced beekeeper.

  • Primary purchase consideration is economic benefit.
  • Honey output is stable in good times but drops sharply when hives are affected.
  • Facing serious challenges such as American foulbrood, bee-eaters, and hive beetles.
  • Has a $200-$500 budget for affordable products for four hives.
  • Produces 1-1.5 kg of honey per hive per week, suggesting a small-scale operation.
  • Interested in practical products that support hive health and productivity.

Jack — Stakeholder/Mechanical Engineering - manufacturing feasibility and practical mechanisms.

  • Highlighted laser reflection accuracy using mirrors for better mite targeting.
  • Found that mechanical solutions can be more affordable than expected.
  • Confirmed the need to balance technical feasibility, precision, and cost effectiveness.
  • Three-axis frames or planar linkages improve precision and mechanical choice.
  • 3D printing can reduce cost and accelerate prototyping.
  • Mechanical component cost around $50 challenges assumptions about custom systems.
  • Balancing innovation, practicality, and affordability is critical in design.

Insight direction: Beekeepers need solutions that are non-invasive, affordable, and deliver clear early-warning value. Trust is built through transparent data, proven accuracy, and minimal disruption to the colony.
